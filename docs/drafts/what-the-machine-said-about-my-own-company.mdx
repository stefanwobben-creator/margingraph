The fair test of a machine that reads accounts is to feed it accounts you
know from the inside. So I uploaded a year of my own: the 2022 annual
accounts of srprs.me, a travel company I co-founded.

It needed one PDF. This is what came back, and what there is to think about
in it.

Turnover up twenty-nine percent. Everyone in the building felt that as
success, because growth is the thing you can feel. What nobody can feel is a
denominator.

## The one line that explains the year

Turnover grew by €873,766. The cost of buying what we sold grew by
€1,060,001.

Read that again, because I had to. **Every extra euro of turnover in 2022
cost €1.21 to buy.** Not to market, not to staff, not to run. Just to buy.
The growth was not diluting our margin, it was negative margin: each
additional sale destroyed money before a single euro of overhead touched it.

And on top of that engine running backwards, marketing spend rose 77% to
€405,120, to win more of exactly those sales. Of every euro of gross profit
the company earned, 66 cents went to winning the next order. The year before
it had been 29.

The company grew itself deeper into the hole, at increasing speed, and the
faster it grew, the better it looked from inside.

## Why the accounts did not shout

Everything above was in the statutory accounts. Nothing was hidden. But a
profit and loss account lists costs in ledger order and measures them against
turnover, and against turnover everything looked survivable: cost of sales
84%, marketing 10%, wages 6%. Percentages of a big and growing number.

Measured against the money that was actually ours, the money left after
buying, the same year reads like an alarm. That difference in arrangement is
the entire idea behind MarginGraph, and 2022 is why I believe it: **the
figures you need to steer a company are not the figures your bookkeeper gives
you.** Not worse figures. The same figures, in a different order, against a
different base.

## What the machine asked me

It also produced the questions it would have sent me into a board meeting
with. The first one:

> Cost of sales took 84 cents of every euro of turnover this period, against
> 73 cents in 2021. What changed there? A good answer is a cause you can
> name: a rate change, a mix shift, a one-off. "Costs went up" is not an
> answer, it is the question restated.

I know now what the answer was. I would have preferred to be asked in March.

## The honest caveats

The machine read one year against another, so it cannot say which of this
was a decision and which of it simply happened; that is exactly the question
it hands back to the owner. It read the profit and loss, not the strategy:
there were reasons for the choices of 2022, and figures do not vote on
reasons. And it read my company with my consent. Every number above is from
the 2022 annual accounts, and I have chosen to publish them because a case
study with the amounts blurred out is an anecdote.
