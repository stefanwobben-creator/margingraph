Substantially. Moving from mostly project revenue to mostly contracted revenue typically adds **one to two turns** to the multiple — on a business earning €500,000, that is €500,000 to €1,000,000 of value.

## Buyers grade revenue in four tiers

<Comparison
  columns={["Tier", "Definition", "Premium"]}
  rows={[
    ["Contracted", "Multi-year, notice period, automatic renewal", "Full"],
    ["Recurring", "Renews by default, cancellable at will", "Most"],
    ["Repeat", "Same customers return, no obligation", "Modest"],
    ["Project", "Won individually each time", "None"],
  ]}
/>

The gap between *contracted* and *repeat* is where most of the value sits, and it is frequently a paperwork problem rather than a commercial one. Customers who have bought monthly for four years will often sign a twelve-month agreement at unchanged pricing.

## The effect is not linear

<Metrics
  items={[
    { label: "0–30% recurring", value: "no premium" },
    { label: "40–70% recurring", value: "+1 to 2 turns", note: "the steep part" },
    { label: "Above 80%", value: "revenue basis", note: "priced on ARR" },
  ]}
/>

Going from 20% to 40% moves little. Going from 45% to 70% can move a great deal — that is the point where recurring revenue covers the fixed cost base.

## What undermines it

Churn. A 5% churn business and a 25% one are not in the same market whatever their recurring percentages say. Present the churn figure yourself; a buyer who has to assume will assume worse than the truth.
